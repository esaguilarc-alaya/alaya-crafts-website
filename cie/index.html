Capability intelligence

Capability, read from the work itself.

Capability is the hardest thing in any organization to see, and the easiest thing to claim. Alaya reads it from accounts of work that was actually done — in a person and in an organization — so that it can be seen, and then cultivated.

The problem nobody is solving

The hardest thing to see is what people can already do.

Ask a capable professional what they are good at and you will get job titles and adjectives. Not because they are hiding anything — because the moment that would tell them, the one where they say I had not thought of that as something I did, only happens when somebody asks properly, and almost nobody ever does.

Ask an organization what it can do and you get an org chart and an inventory of declared skills. The same gap, one floor up, with a larger budget behind it.

Both sides are guessing about the same thing from opposite ends, and neither has an instrument.

The cost of that is quiet, which is why it survives. Development budget aimed at capability that was already there. People hired from outside for work somebody inside had done twice. Projects not attempted because nobody knew the company could do them. None of it shows up as a loss anywhere, because the thing that would have made it visible has never existed.

None of this is a data problem. The evidence exists — it is in what people did last quarter and the quarter before. It has simply never been read.

What Alaya does

Capability is cultivated, not counted.

It is not a stock sitting in a warehouse waiting to be inventoried. It grows through craft — practice done deliberately, repeatedly, under real conditions. Naming it does nothing for it. Scoring it does less.

But cultivation needs a direction, and direction needs sight. You cannot grow what you cannot see, and you cannot see it in a list of claims.

So Alaya reads accounts of work that was actually done, on two axes at once.

Practices are not job titles — a practice is classified by what the act changed. One piece of work usually belongs to several at once, and is allowed to. Dimensions are the same everywhere; what changes with the craft is the practice they show up in.

Capability is what sits at the intersection. Neither axis means anything on its own, and a rating means less than either.

That intersection is what makes it usable. “Advanced” gives nobody anything to do. Knowing that someone's strength shows up in the reasoning and relational aspects of work that changes what is settled — and thins out where the work is about holding something steady — tells a person where to push next and tells an organization what to develop, where to deploy, and where it is exposed.

And every reading stays anchored to the exact sentence in the account that supports it. No sentence, no reading.

The difference is not speed. It is where the instrument points.

Why there is a market

The flood is the reason, not the obstacle.

Hundreds of funded companies say they are fixing exactly this problem, for organizations and professionals both — automating the writing of résumés on one side, automating the parsing of them on the other, and matching skills matrices to job descriptions in between.

What almost none of them touch is the thing being written and parsed. AI is being applied, very fast and very well, to a faulty process and to three unusually thin representations: the résumé, the job description, and the skills matrix. Not one of those was ever a decent description of what a person can do. Automating them in both directions does not make them one — it makes the same thin thing arrive sooner, in greater quantity, from both sides at once.

Applying intelligence to a thin representation returns a thin answer faster. The representation was the problem.

When a signal becomes free to produce, it stops being a signal. The only thing that recovers is something more expensive to fake. Constructing a whole situation — what it was like before, who objected, what it cost — is not something anyone does by accident.

And the inward problem is larger than the hiring one and much less served. Organizations are building internal skills inventories at a serious rate, and the research behind that trend keeps reporting the same thing: the visibility gap is not closing. Cataloguing declared skills faster does not tell a company who can actually do the work. It is the résumé problem again, indoors, with a bigger budget behind it.

Meanwhile the question do we already have someone who can do this has become expensive to get wrong. Hiring externally for capability that already exists inside is a cost nobody can see, because the thing that would make it visible has never existed.

The engine

CIE — the Capability Intelligence Engine.

Everything below is a way of putting the same machine in the hands of an organization or a professional. It has two parts, and the line between them is the whole design.

One · the conversation

An AI instance, on a short leash

It asks about work that was actually done — a situation, what the person did, how it ended — and chases the specifics people skip past without noticing: what it was like before, how many, what made it hard, who had to agree.

Its scope is deliberately bounded. It cannot ask anyone to rate themselves, and it cannot ask anyone to agree with an interpretation. It gathers. It does not judge.

Two · the reading

A backend that recognises a landscape

What comes out of the conversation is read against a capability landscape Alaya built: practices, which describe where capability is exercised, and dimensions, which describe how it shows when it is.

Every reading is anchored to a quoted sentence from the account, or it returns nothing. Nothing is inferred about a person that cannot be pointed at.
